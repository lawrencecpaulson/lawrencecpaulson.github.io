My first ever "[blog post](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~lp15/Pages/Scream.html)" (though not in this blog) was about how to write a research paper. 
I wrote it after reviewing a batch of particularly badly-written papers for a conference, probably ITP or CADE. 
Later – a couple of years ago – came the campaign to oust Claudine Gay as President of Harvard on the grounds that she had committed plagiarism.
Many allegations of plagiarism were flying about; billionaire hedge fund manager Bill Ackman,
one of Gay's key opponents, 
discovered that alleging plagiarism was a game that [anyone could play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Ackman). 
In all this, it suddenly dawned on me that many of my complaints about the papers I was reviewing were really about plagiarism. 
It's a tricky concept to pin down, which makes it especially convenient as a tool to ease out people who happen to be the wrong sex or color. 
And with AI, it is automated. 

### The basics of plagiarism

Plagiarism is generally defined as using another person's work without acknowledgement, 
passing it off as your own. 
A common situation is when a student who has to complete an assignment
copies another student's work. 
In the Cambridge Computer Lab, we have long followed the practice of asking students 
to bring their programming assignment for a brief interview to answer a few questions about their code. 
If they didn't write it, they won't know anything about it. 
Getting answers from [Stack Exchange](https://stackexchange.com) also counts as plagiarism. 
A particularly informative case was when a student, 
asked to write a literature review of certain papers, 
simply copied and pasted paragraphs out of each one. 
Asked to explain himself at a disciplinary tribunal, he simply said, 
"I've always done this and no one ever cared before."
We care because he didn't do his assignment, and he didn't learn anything from the papers either. 
But how does this relate to the badly written papers I was grumbling about? 

[Recollections may vary](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2021/mar/10/recollections-may-vary-how-the-papers-covered-queens-response-to-meghan-interview), 
but it seemed to me that many of the papers I read back in 2015 (and before and since), have begun by referring to three things. 

* the Pentium FDIV bug
* the Ariane 5 crash
* "Program testing can be used to show the presence of bugs, but never to show their absence!"

(I saw the last one quoted again in a technical document only a month ago.)

This is a curious list. The Pentium bug and the Ariane 5 crash were both high profile disasters, but they had also happened in the mid-'90s, about 20 years earlier. 
Neither was especially relevant to formal verification. 
The first was a fabrication error: 
missing entries in a lookup table for floating-point division. 
The second was caused by the use of old code from Ariane 4. 
The third, that famous quote by Edsger Dijkstra, 
is obvious to anyone who ever tried to get a computer to do anything since about 1950. 
It's not insightful, original or even pithy.
It is not "[These are the times that try men's souls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_American_Crisis)".
It is not even [Pigs are much bigger than you expect](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zAYR-i4S-ZE). 

But is it plagiarism? 
A paragraph mentioning these items clearly must not be copied verbatim from elsewhere, 
nor paraphrased: both are straightforward plagiarism. 
But there are grey areas. 
What if you copy all the references cited in someone's paragraph, 
as well as the specific claims made about them, weaving them into a new and original paragraph?
If you have never looked up those sources, 
if you don't know whether they back those claims, 
if you don't even know whether those references exist, 
then (to paraphrase Jesus) you have committed plagiarism in your heart. 
It can't be a coincidence that people keep making the same bad choices. 
And finally, for a paper submitted to a conference on verification, 
all of these arguments are preaching to the converted. 
You can be accused of plagiarism even if the relevant material is entirely redundant. 

E. W. Dijkstra. Notes on structured programming. In O.-J. Dahl, E. W. Dijkstra, and C. A. R.
Hoare, editors, *Structured Programming*. Academic Press, 1972.


A factor of a thousand is already far beyond our powers of imagination!
